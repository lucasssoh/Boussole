**"My teacher gave us their slides. Can I share them?"** Received does not mean yours.

In fifteen minutes, this session shows you:

- who a course document belongs to;
- what you may do with it for yourself;
- what changes the moment you share it.

:::note
This track gives general information, not legal advice. Rules vary from one country to another,
and your school or university may have its own. When in doubt, ask the author or your
institution.
:::

## A document belongs to its author {#ownership}

Slides, lecture notes, exercise sheets, past exams: someone wrote them. Often your teacher,
sometimes a team, sometimes a publisher whose figures appear in the slides.

That work is protected by :term[copyright]{id=copyright}:

- it applies **from the moment the work is written**: no form, no registration, no © symbol
  needed;
- it belongs to the **author**, or to the institution they work for, depending on the country
  and on their contract;
- it protects the **form**: the text, the slides, the figures, the choice and order of the
  examples. Not the ideas: a theorem, a fact or a method belongs to no one.

| You received… | You own… | You do not own… |
|---|---|---|
| a PDF of the lecture notes | your copy of the file | the right to publish it |
| a book you bought | the paper | the right to photocopy it for the class |
| a film you bought | the disc or the file | the right to put it on a video site |

:::tip
Receiving a document gives you a copy, not the rights on it.
:::

::::mcq{#c1-a1}
Your teacher emailed the class a PDF of their lecture notes, with no © symbol on it. Who holds
the rights on it?

- [ ] Nobody, since there is no © symbol
  > Copyright applies as soon as the work is written: no symbol is needed.
- [ ] Every student who received it
  > Receiving a copy does not give the rights on it.
- [x] Its author, or the institution they work for

:::hint
Read the first point of the list again: when does copyright start?
:::

:::solution
**Its author, or their institution.** Copyright applies from the moment the work is written,
with or without ©. Receiving the PDF gives you a copy, not the rights.
:::
::::

## For yourself, or for others {#private-use}

**"I am building a track from my teacher's slides: is that allowed?"** For yourself, in general,
yes.

Two very different uses:

| For yourself | For others |
|---|---|
| You study with the documents you were given. | You send, post or publish them. |
| You make notes, summaries, a :term[track]{id=track} on your machine. | To a classmate, a group chat, a website, a shared drive. |
| That is what the documents were given to you for. | You need the right to share: the author's consent, or a licence that allows it. |

Boussole works on your machine: a track you build stays in your folder. Nothing leaves it unless
**you** send it.

:::warning
"It is only for one friend" is still sharing. The size of the audience does not change who
decides: the author.
:::

::::mcq{#c1-a2 multiple=true}
Which of these situations are **sharing**?

- [ ] Building a track from the slides, for your own revision
  > The track stays on your machine: that is use for yourself.
- [x] Sending the slides to a classmate who missed the lecture
- [x] Posting the track, slides included, on a course-sharing website
- [ ] Printing the lecture notes to write on them
  > The print stays with you: that is use for yourself.

:::hint
For each one, ask yourself: does the document leave your hands?
:::

:::solution
As long as the document stays with you (a track, a print, your notes), it is use **for
yourself**. As soon as it reaches someone else, even one classmate, it is **sharing**.
:::
::::

## Who decides, and whom to ask {#who-decides}

**"So who can tell me yes?"** The person who holds the rights.

1. **The author**: your teacher, in most cases. Many say yes, often with a condition: "within
   the class only", "keep my name on it".
2. **Your institution**: its rules on course material, often in the student charter or on the
   course page.
3. **The document itself**: a licence written on it may already say yes. That is the next
   session.

Countries differ on the details, and on the exceptions, such as those for teaching or for
private copies. This track keeps to what holds almost everywhere: the author decides who may
share their work.

:::example[Asking before sharing]
Inès built a track from the lecture notes of her networks teacher. A classmate asks for it.

She writes to the teacher: "I built revision sessions from your notes with Boussole. May I share
them with the class? Your name is on them."

The teacher answers: "Yes, within the class, not online." Inès shares the track with the class,
and only with the class.
:::

::::mcq{#c1-a3}
A classmate asks for the track you built from your teacher's slides. What do you do first?

- [ ] Send it: the teacher already gave the slides to the whole class
  > The teacher gave them to read, not to pass on in another form: that is the teacher's call.
- [x] Ask the teacher whether you may share it, and on what terms
- [ ] Remove the teacher's name, so that nobody can complain
  > That hides who did the work: it makes things worse, not better.

:::hint
Read the list again: who is the first person to ask?
:::

:::solution
**Ask the teacher.** They hold the rights on the slides your track is built from. Many agree,
often with a condition, such as sharing within the class only.
:::
::::
